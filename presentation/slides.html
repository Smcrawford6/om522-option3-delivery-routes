The decision

Sadie Crawford

Who decides, and what

Every morning a dispatcher at one depot assigns each customer to a vehicle and chooses the order of stops on each route, before any driver leaves.

What makes it hard

  • Capacity: a route's total demand cannot exceed the vehicle's capacity.
  • Time windows: service must start inside the window. Arriving early means waiting; arriving late is not allowed.
  • Shifts: leave at shift start and return by shift end.
  • Every customer is served exactly once.

How we score a plan

  • Primary: total travel time (excludes waiting and service).
  • Secondary: vehicles used.
  • Plus computation time.

Our added assumption

A route may not cross itself on the map. A driver should never cut back across their own path. Different routes may cross. Travel time equals straight-line distance here, so a self-crossing is a real detour.

Method 1: the rule to beat

Sadie Crawford

Nearest feasible neighbor, one vehicle at a time

  1. Take vehicles in identifier order; each leaves at its shift start.
  2. Drive to the nearest unserved customer (ties by ID) that
    · fits the remaining capacity,
    · can start service by its window end,
    · still allows a return by the shift end.
  3. No customer qualifies → return; the next vehicle starts.
  4. Anyone left unserved → “no feasible decision found”.

Deterministic, no seed. Implemented exactly as written, so it does not apply our crossing rule: 8 self-crossings on the large case.

Method 2: the researched method

Zacc Gleason
1

Construct

Solomon (1987) I1 insertion. Seed a route, then insert the customer with the best c2 = λ·t(depot,u) − c1, where c1 blends the added travel and the push-forward of later stops. Every window is checked on insertion. 12 parameter starts (α1 × λ × seed rule).

2

Improve

Local search with the brief's moves: relocate 1–3 customers (or-opt), exchange between routes, reverse a segment (2-opt), and swap route tails (2-opt*). Candidate moves use each customer's 12 nearest neighbors.

3

Ruin & recreate

Large neighborhood search (Shaw 1998; Ropke & Pisinger 2006): remove 4–30% of customers, reinsert by regret-2 or greedy insertion, improve, and accept by simulated annealing. 4 seeded chains; keep the best.

1,683.4rule to beat
→
1,114.4best of 12 I1 starts + local search
→
1,071.2+ ruin and recreate (final)

Total travel (min) on development_medium after each stage.

Representation: one ordered list of customers per vehicle; each route caches its load, service starts, and the latest feasible start at every stop, so a window check for an insertion takes constant time.

Stopping rule: 4 chains × 2,000 iterations (fixed count). The 480 s limit is only a safety cap and is never hit. Seed 522 → chain c uses Random(100·seed + c); reruns are identical (tested).

  • Reads only the input files and decision.json.
  • Recomputes every arrival, service start, return, load, and measure from the visit lists.

Recommend

Adopt the researched method

Solomon I1 + local search + ruin and recreate, with the no-self-crossing rule.

Expected benefit

−612 min/day

36% less planned driving on the 45-customer day (1,683.4 → 1,071.2 min), 3 fewer vehicles (11 → 8), and about 593 fewer minutes on an average uncertain day.

Tradeoff accepted

Tighter routes: about 40 s of computing instead of milliseconds, and slightly more days with at least one late stop (7 vs 6 of 30).

Would change if…

average congestion runs ≥ 10% above the planning data and any missed window on a day is unacceptable. Then neither fixed plan works as is: plan with buffered travel times or replan during the day.

Limitations, and thank you

Zacc Gleason

Limitations

  • A heuristic: no proof of optimality. Different seeds land within about 1% of each other on 120 customers.
  • It plans for exact travel times and builds in no slack, which is why it is sensitive to heavier congestion.
  • It minimizes travel only; using fewer vehicles is a side effect, not a target.
  • Evidence comes from four synthetic instances from one generator, plus one infeasible edge case.
  • No-crossing is our rule. If no crossing-free plan exists, the method reports none even when a crossing plan would exist.
